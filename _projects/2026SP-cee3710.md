As part of my Structural Modeling class spring of my junior year, we were tasked to work in groups to design a truss bridge with the best strength to weight ratio. We were provided the base members of our bridge, and could design it to be up to 5 inches tall with as many members as we desired.

## Choosing the Design

![Sketch of Truss]({{ "/assets/images/bridgedwg.png" | relative_url }}){: .inline-image-l}

Within my team of three, we decided we would each create what we thought was an optimal design and choose the best of the three designs to build.

To create my version, I drew out as many truss configurations as I could, which resulted in approximately 15 different designs. Using MASTAN, I tested the designs to determine which had the least members in compression along with the highest compression to force applied ratio. I chose this method to narrow down my design as the material we were provided had a far lower compressive yield strength compared to its tensile yield strength.

After meeting as a group, I took all of the designs and created a <a href="https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1sgKjIH2O8lgBz2FE79M2ZglDg2o2MgH8zLeSQ1g41Jo/edit?usp=sharing">master spreadsheet</a>. This spreadsheet contained the stresses in each member for an applied force of 1 pound, as well as the yield load for each member and the required size. We decided to size all members to fail with the same applied load, and the limiting factor was the provided base members that we were unable to change. From there, using provided weights and densities, an estimated weight of each design was calculated along with the maximum force that could be applied and the ratio was calculated. We picked the design with the highest ratio, as that was the design goal our professor provided. 

## Designing the Connections

While sizing the members was a simple math problem, we also needed to provide CAD models. Being the only mechanical engineer in the group, as well as the only one with previous CAD experience, I volunteered to create the members in Fusion 360 so they could be 3D printed. 

![CAD model of connection piece]({{ "/assets/images/connection.png" | relative_url }}){: .inline-image-l}

We were provided files with parameters that made adjusting the length and width of each member simple. The challenge came from designing the connection plates for each member. We were required to have all holes placed a certain distance apart to maintain structural integrity while also ensuring all members shared a common orgin for their axes. This took quite a bit of experimenting to ensure the requirements were met while also ensuring there would be no interferance that would prohibit us from actually building the design. Some adjustments had to be made to certain angles and member lengths to ensure everything fit, but after some trial and error our design was finally completed.

## Testing and Results

![assembled bridge]({{ "/assets/images/bridge2.png" | relative_url }}){: .inline-image-l}

The mass of the assembled bridge was 0.7148 kg, which was over double the estimated mass. This was due to multiple factors including different bolt weights than assumed, neglecting to include connection plates in the calculation, and not accounting for the added material to the top and bottom members to hold the bridge together. 

To test the bridge, it was loaded into a machine that steadily increased the downward force applied on the middle of the bridge until failute. The bridge failed at an an applied force of 373.9 lb (169.6 kg), which give the bridge a load to weight ratio of 273.3. 

![broken bridge]({{ "/assets/images/break.png" | relative_url }}){: .inline-image-l}

The initial failure point on the bridge was one of the connection plates at the bolt, which then caused one of the base members to break at the bolt hole. If I were to do this project again, I would increase the material around the holes on the connection plates to prevent tear out and increase the chance of a member failing to have a more accurate gauge of how successful the truss configuration was.
